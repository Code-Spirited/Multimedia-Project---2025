The universe is about 13.8 billion years old, filled with billions upon billions of galaxies. Among them is one special galaxy called the Milky Way. Inside this galaxy sits a giant, blazing ball of fire called the Sun. Orbiting it, at a distance of about 150 million kilometers, is a beautiful, one-of-a-kind planet that is 4.5 billion years old. It's unique because it holds life — and everything needed to sustain that life.
Nature shaped the course of life on Earth, creating thousands of different forms. Somewhere along this journey, the smartest species the world has ever seen was born: Homo sapiens — humans. Born roughly 300,000 years ago, this clever species made invention after invention for its own comfort and safety, building a secure world to live in. Over time, humanity spread across the entire globe. But humanity had no idea that one day it would become a terrifying threat — not just to itself, but to every form of life on Earth.
In the race for progress and power, humans have taken on an incredibly destructive risk — one whose shadow has been pushing the world toward disaster for years. That risk is the climate crisis.
To understand the climate crisis, we first need to understand a few basics.
Most people mix up the ideas of weather and climate, so the first step is understanding the difference between the two.
Weather is the condition of the air above a specific place at a specific time — things like wind direction, rainfall, or how hot or cold it is.
Weather keeps changing, sometimes from day to day, sometimes hour by hour.
Weather is what you feel right now, what's happening in front of your eyes — pouring rain one moment, blazing afternoon sun the next.
Climate, on the other hand, is the average of these conditions — wind, rainfall, heat, and cold — measured consistently across a region over decades or centuries.
For example, tropical regions are usually hot and stay quite wet, with heavy rainfall throughout the year.
Polar regions, by contrast, have very long, harsh winters and only brief, mild summers.
When scientists talk about climate, they look at rainfall, temperature, humidity, sunlight, wind, and other such measurements, and work out the average for a specific region over a long stretch of time — at least 30 years.
Earth is estimated to be about 4.5 billion years old, and over that long history, its climate has changed many times. Around 50 million years ago, it was so warm that the Arctic Circle had palm trees and crocodiles instead of ice.
Earth's climate has always changed naturally, and it will keep changing. But today's climate change is not one of those natural shifts. The real worry isn't that the climate is changing — it's how fast it's changing. In the past, climate shifts took thousands, even millions, of years.
This time, the shift in the global climate has happened in barely 200 years. And the truth is, the culprit this time is no one but us. For the first time, climate change is being driven by human activity, and it's happening so fast that it has stunned scientists. What's even more frightening is that these changes are bringing destructive consequences — putting not just humans, but countless other species, on the brink of disaster.
The scale of the damage is plain to see today.
Ice is melting rapidly.
Droughts and floods have become more frequent, destroying crops and wiping out entire communities.
Extreme weather events, like storms and cyclones, have increased.
A warmer climate means wildfires start more easily and burn for longer.
Since 1870, sea levels have risen by 21 to 24 centimeters — and that number is only expected to grow in the years ahead.
The changing climate is destroying animal habitats — for many species, survival itself has become a struggle.
For thousands of years, humanity lived on this planet without causing much harm to it.
But then, starting in the 1750s, the Industrial Revolution changed everything.
This period of massive change began in Britain about two hundred and fifty years ago, and it spread across the entire world in no time.
Before that, most people made their living from farming, and everyday goods were made by hand, one at a time. But once huge steam-powered machines were invented, production shot up dramatically. These large factories began burning enormous amounts of fossil fuels for energy.
Millions of people left their villages and moved to booming industrial cities in search of work.
In 1750, only 15% of England's population lived in cities. By 1900, that number had climbed to 85%.
Meanwhile, factories multiplied, metal-making industries grew, and railway networks spread across the land. All of this required burning huge amounts of coal to power steam engines. Then came motor vehicles and newer machines, whose hunger for fuel burned through even more fossil fuels. All this burning released poisonous gases into the air.
At the same time, the human population was growing fast. More land was needed, so forests were cut down to make room for farmland to feed everyone. But along with food, the demand for clothes and other goods also grew — leading to even more factories.
The result? More burning, more smoke, and more toxic gases released into the sky.
All of this activity combined to suddenly shift the climate of the entire planet.
But how exactly are these gases harmful once they're released into the atmosphere, and why?
To understand this, we need to know about an interesting natural phenomenon: the greenhouse effect.
The air in Earth's atmosphere is actually a mixture of different gases.
It's made up of about 78% nitrogen, 21% oxygen, 0.93% argon, a small amount of water vapor, and trace amounts of other gases like carbon dioxide and methane.
Earth's atmosphere is made up of five layers. The closest one to us is the troposphere, which extends 7 to 15 kilometers above the surface. This is where clouds form, where weather happens, and where almost all greenhouse gases are found.
Besides providing the gases we need to breathe, Earth's atmosphere also plays several other important roles.
For instance, the atmosphere protects us from the sun's harmful ultraviolet (UV) radiation.
Much of this protection comes from the ozone layer, high up in the stratosphere.
This layer acts like a shield, absorbing the most harmful UV radiation from the sun and keeping it from reaching the ground.
Another major benefit of our atmosphere is that it gives us extra warmth.
Of the solar radiation that reaches Earth, about 30% bounces off the atmosphere and back into space. The rest passes through and is absorbed by the oceans and land surface, and some of it radiates back upward as infrared radiation.
Some of this infrared radiation escapes directly into space, but a good portion is absorbed by greenhouse gases in the atmosphere. This is what raises Earth's temperature. This process is called the greenhouse effect — and it's actually not a bad thing.
In fact, it's a blessing — it's what keeps Earth's temperature at a level where life can survive. Sunlight and the greenhouse effect work together to make Earth livable. Without it, our planet would be a frozen, barren, uninhabitable ball of ice. Scientists estimate that without the greenhouse effect, Earth's average temperature would be around -18°C.
But too much of anything is harmful. The more greenhouse gases there are, the more Earth's average temperature rises. And unfortunately, that's exactly what's happening today.
Of all the greenhouse gases in the atmosphere, carbon dioxide makes up the largest share at 76%, followed by methane at 16%, nitrous oxide at 6%, and fluorinated gases at 2%.
Of all the gases humans have added to the atmosphere, carbon dioxide is by far the biggest contributor.
Once it enters the air, the carbon cycle slowly removes some of it — but this happens at a snail's pace.
That means much of the carbon dioxide being released today will still be floating in the atmosphere centuries from now.
For most of the past 800,000 years, atmospheric CO₂ levels stayed between roughly 180 and 280 parts per million (ppm) — a time when human civilization, which is barely 10,000 years old, didn't even exist yet.
But in just the last few decades, the picture has completely changed. Remarkably, half of all the CO₂ ever produced by humans has been released in just the past 30 years.
By 2013, after decades of burning fossil fuels and clearing forests, atmospheric CO₂ levels had crossed 400 ppm — a level not seen on this planet in millions of years. Today, in 2025, that number has climbed past 420 ppm.
When we talk about climate change, the biggest culprit is carbon emissions.
Looking at global carbon emissions by sector, the largest share comes from electricity and heat production — 16.7 billion tons, about a third of total global emissions. Powering homes, factories, and cities with fossil fuels remains the single biggest offender.
The second-largest source is transportation, at 8.2 billion tons a year. Cars, trucks, ships, and planes still burn enormous amounts of fuel, pushing emissions even higher.
By country, China ranks first, followed by the US, with India in third place.
Just these three countries together are responsible for more than half of the world's carbon emissions. But not everyone shares the blame equally.
According to a 2023 OXFAM report, the richest 10% of the world's population is responsible for half of all global carbon emissions. Even more strikingly, the richest 1% alone account for 16% of emissions — roughly the same as the emissions of the poorest 66% of humanity, about 5 billion people.
A single billionaire can emit more carbon in a year than an ordinary person would in over a thousand years. Even a short trip on a private jet can release more carbon than an average person does in an entire year.
What's especially unfortunate is that the people hit hardest by the climate crisis are often the ones who contributed to it the least.
But a question remains: when and how did the reality of the climate crisis first come to light?
To find out, we need to go back 200 years, to a time when humans knew nothing about the greenhouse effect. The first hint came in the 1820s, when French mathematician and physicist Joseph Fourier examined Earth's temperature. He calculated that our planet, sitting about 150 million kilometers from the sun, shouldn't be as warm as it actually was.
The implication was clear — some invisible force was trapping heat.
In 1856, American scientist Eunice Foote demonstrated that both CO₂ and water vapor absorb far more heat than ordinary air.
Forty years later, chemist Svante Arrhenius firmly established the link between atmospheric carbon dioxide levels and Earth's rising temperature — more CO₂ means more heat.
In the 1990s, ice cores drilled out of Antarctica gave scientists a record of atmospheric CO₂ levels stretching back 800,000 years. These long, narrow cylinders are drilled deep into ancient ice. By examining the tiny bubbles of prehistoric air trapped inside them, scientists can work out what the atmosphere's composition was like thousands of centuries ago. Remarkably, throughout that entire span, CO₂ levels never once rose above 300 ppm.
Every year, thousands of atmospheric samples are collected to measure gas levels. Changing temperatures on land, in the air, and in the oceans are tracked by scientific stations around the world and by satellites orbiting the planet.
All these measurements paint a clear picture: greenhouse gas levels in the atmosphere are rising, and so is our planet's average temperature.
The overwhelming majority of the world's climate scientists — 97%, to be precise — agree that the climate crisis is real and that humans are causing it.
Since the 1990s, the world has been kept informed about the scale of the climate crisis by the IPCC — the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change — a group of the world's leading volunteer scientists, established by the United Nations in 1988. Every year, its expert scientists review thousands of research studies, and every four to seven years, they release a comprehensive report aimed at pushing policymakers to take effective action against the threat of climate change.
The IPCC's warnings began with its First Assessment Report in 1990, which successfully predicted the pace of global warming.
The Second Assessment Report, released in 1995, stated with greater confidence that human activity was indeed the real cause of climate change. This report became the foundation for the 1997 Kyoto Protocol — the landmark agreement that, for the first time, required developed nations to cut their greenhouse gas emissions.
Three more reports followed, in 2001, 2007, and 2014, clearly and convincingly showing that greenhouse gas emissions had reached record highs, and that global temperatures were rising at an unprecedented rate. The conclusion was clear: humans were the main driver behind the recent warming.
As a result, the 2014 Fifth Assessment Report shook the world, leading to COP21 in 2015, where 196 countries adopted the Paris Agreement — a landmark achievement in international cooperation on climate change. This agreement, which took effect in 2016, set the goal of limiting global temperature rise to no more than 1.5°C above pre-industrial levels (the average global temperature between 1850 and 1900).
According to the IPCC's Special Report on Global Warming of 1.5°C, keeping warming to 1.5°C — with little or no overshoot — requires cutting global CO₂ emissions by about 45% by 2030 compared to 2010 levels, and reaching net-zero by 2050.
But recent records make it clear that we are failing to meet this goal. Making matters worse, UN weather experts at the World Meteorological Organization have announced the alarming news that 2024 was the hottest year on record globally, with temperatures reaching 1.55°C above pre-industrial levels.
So why does this 1.5°C threshold matter so much? What happens if we cross it?
Scientists warn that if temperatures rise beyond 1.5°C, it could trigger dangerous climate tipping points — changes that, once set in motion, keep building on themselves and can never be reversed. Scientists have identified 16 such tipping points.

All of this sounds painful and discouraging to hear. But the good news is, we already know how to solve this problem.
In the fight against the climate crisis, our biggest enemy is the carbon dioxide humans emit. Every effort we make should be focused on reducing it and reaching net-zero as quickly as possible.
One key solution for tackling climate change is shifting to renewable energy. Renewables are virtually limitless energy sources capable of meeting human energy needs indefinitely — these include solar, wind, hydro, and nuclear power.
Beyond that, some well-known solutions include stopping deforestation and expanding tree cover on a large scale, cutting down on consumerism, reducing meat consumption in favor of a plant-based diet (especially grains like millets), adopting energy-efficient solutions like electric vehicles and LEDs, improving waste management, and using public transport.
In 2017, researchers from Lund University published a peer-reviewed paper titled "The Climate Mitigation Gap" in the journal Environmental Research Letters. The study reviewed 39 peer-reviewed papers and identified four of the most effective actions for cutting carbon dioxide emissions: having one fewer child, living car-free, avoiding air travel, and eating a plant-based diet.
The study called these four "High-Impact Actions," and found that the most effective one — having one fewer child — could prevent roughly eight times more carbon dioxide than all the other three actions combined.
The time has come for us to understand how our everyday choices affect both ourselves and nature — a bond we share and cannot break. We need to rein in the actions that are harming our planet and do everything possible to protect it. Because only when the Earth is safe can life continue to thrive on this remarkable planet.
